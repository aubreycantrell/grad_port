The Design Problem

Most AI creativity tools automate execution, they make things for you. The real question is whether AI can instead expand what you consider, without taking over. In spaces like collage, where the creation process is physically and thematically rich, how can AI inspire creation while preserving user autonomy?

What I Built & Found

I designed the Collage Collaborator: a human-AI co-creation system that generates visual suggestions without finalizing outcomes. A study with 30 participants showed AI significantly increased material exploration while participants retained full creative ownership over personal themes and direction.

Published at ACM IUI '26 →

The Design Challenge

Generative AI is increasingly embedded in creative tools, but most implementations prioritize output speed over user agency. They produce finished artifacts rather than supporting the messy, iterative, deeply personal process of making something.

How might we design an AI system that expands what a creator considers, without deciding what they make?

Prior research had shown that exposure to AI-generated content can actually reduce creative fluency: users anchor to what they've seen and explore less. I wanted to know if the right design could reverse that effect. The hypothesis was that physical collage, where you can't paste a digital output directly onto paper, would create a productive friction that forced interpretation rather than imitation.

Research Approach

Research Questions

RQ1: How does interaction with AI impact participants’ creative exploration? Specifically, how does AI influence divergence from initial ideas and willingness to explore new materials, colors, themes, or visual strategies?

RQ2: How did participants engage with AI-generated collage suggestions?

RQ2a: To what extent did participants adopt, reinterpret, or resist AI-generated suggestions?

RQ2b: Which types of generated collages inspired the most change in participant work?

RQ2c: How did participants attribute creative decisions to AI in their reflections?

Study Design

This is a within-subjects, before-after study, where each participant serves as their own baseline, which strengthens causal inference despite a modest sample size: the goal was to understand not just what changed in participant's collages, but how and why they engaged with or pushed back on AI suggestions.

I chose a mixed-methods approach because divergent thinking can't be directly observed. Quantitative proxies like semantic distance and material diversity capture the breadth of creative exploration; thematic coding of written reflections captures the depth: the personal reasoning, the moments of inspiration, and the moments of intentional rejection.

Methods

Quantitative

Paired-samples t-tests on behavioral measures before and after AI use, comparing each participant against their own baseline.

Behavioral

Total Additions & New Materials

Count of discrete physical elements added and new material types introduced after AI use — operationalizing material fluency and breadth of exploration.

NLP

Semantic Distance (NLTK)

Semantic distance between newly introduced materials and existing collage elements, averaged per iteration. Higher scores indicate more conceptually diverse additions.

Computer Vision

Color Diversity (CIELAB)

Dominant colors extracted via k-means clustering in CIELAB color space. Color diversity index computed as weighted mean pairwise perceptual distance between palette colors.

Spatial

Compositional Space Usage

Collage surface divided into a 3×3 grid. Additions tracked per quadrant across tasks to detect localized changes in spatial engagement.

NLP

Written Reflection Analysis

Word count and semantic distance of written check-in responses before and after AI use, measuring changes in descriptive engagement and conceptual range.

Survey

Creative Self-Efficacy & AI Attitudes

Likert pre-surveys on Creative Self-Efficacy and attitudes toward AI in art, analyzed via Pearson correlation to understand how prior orientation moderated AI engagement.

Qualitative

Dual-coder thematic analysis of open-ended reflections, independently coded for how and why participants engaged with or pushed back on AI suggestions.

Discussion Analysis

Open Response Check-ins

Check ins at each stage of the study that asked users to descibe their collage and their additions. Divided into Adoption (directly implementing AI recommendations), Inspiration (partially adopting AI recommendations or expressing inspiration), and Rejection (expressing dislike for AI recommendations or no sign of implementation of suggestions).

The System

The Collage Collaborator generates visual suggestions layered onto a user's in-progress collage. It never edits or finalizes the physical work; it only proposes, and the user decides what to do with it.

Variation types

The five presets intentionally span different creative dimensions: materials, form, texture, and theme, so no single suggestion dominates attention and users are pushed to compare rather than simply accept or reject.

New Materials

Fabric, metallic foil, iridescent film, feathers, yarn, pompoms — tactile elements not present in the original.

Contemporary Shapes

Contrasting colors, geometric forms, pen strokes, and chromatic emphasis layered over the composition.

Paper & Texture

Scraps, layered surfaces, torn edges — traditional collage materials adding depth and texture.

Thematic Playful

Humorous or unexpected objects and imagery that interact with and subvert existing elements.

Thematic Reinforcing

Symbols, motifs, or imagery that amplify and extend the collage's existing visual narrative.

Custom

User-directed generation — specify medium, theme, imagery, or partial ideas to steer the AI toward a specific conceptual direction.

What We Found

Across four significant quantitative measures, AI use expanded participant's creative exploration. Critically, this diverges from prior work showing AI exposure reduces fluency, the design decision to maintain separation between suggestion and implementation appears to be the mechanism that made the difference.

AI's Influence Was Dimension-Specific

There were no significant changes in thematic diversity, color diversity, or global composition after AI use. This is an important finding: It suggests that AI expanded participant's material vocabulary, concrete, distinct options the system could surface, but did not touch their personal themes, which involve more abstract and emotionally rooted connections that resist external suggestion. The AI helped with how participants expressed their collage; it did not change what they were expressing.

Three Distinct Engagement Patterns

Rather than a binary adopt-or-ignore relationship with AI suggestions, participants fell into three qualitatively distinct patterns:

9/30
Adapted

Directly implemented AI suggestions: specific materials, forms, or placements credited to the Collage Collaborator. Custom prompting had the highest adaptation rate of any generation type.

12/30
Reinterpreted

Used AI as a springboard, not a blueprint. Described being "inspired" but made the idea their own, different materials, different placement, same conceptual spark.

9/30
Resisted

Expressed deliberate skepticism: concerns about originality, AI's inability to understand personal meaning, and observed racial bias in generations. Resistance was articulate, not passive.

The Resistance Finding

The 9 participants who pushed back on AI suggestions are one of the most interesting findings in the study. Their critiques were substantive: one participant explained that their collage represented their relationship with their sister and they didn't want AI to influence it because "it doesn't understand my relationship with my sister." Another noted racial bias in generated imagery.

These aren't failed interactions, but users engaging critically with a system and making deliberate authorial decisions. The paper argues, as a design implication rather than a measured outcome, that resistance should be designed for, not designed away. Making rejection as easy as adoption, and treating skepticism as signal rather than failure, is the recommendation.

Experience Level Moderated Effects

Arts-experienced participants showed significantly stronger material semantic distance effects (p=.043), suggesting AI most effectively expanded the material vocabulary of people who already had a developed creative framework to filter suggestions through.

Non-arts students showed localized compositional changes in one specific grid quadrant, suggesting they used AI as an entry point for spatial decision-making rather than material exploration.

Design Principles for AI Creativity Tools

The study produced Five Principles for Designing AI Systems that support divergent thinking without diminishing creative agency, where each is grounded in a specific finding or observed behavior from the study.

01

Design for Interpretation, Not Automation

Maintain separation between AI suggestion and human implementation. Every suggestion should require active translation: that friction is what preserves agency and prevents fixation.

02

Generate Simultaneous Diverse Alternatives

Presenting five distinct variations at once reduced anchoring to any single suggestion. Structured diversity, spanning different creative dimensions, outperforms iterative single-output generation.

03

Provide Dimension-Specific Support

AI is effective for concrete, discrete dimensions like materials. Abstract, personally meaningful dimensions like theme require different support, perhaps reflective prompts rather than visual generations.

04

Support Both Breadth and Depth Modes

The Collage Collaborator supported breadth well, exposing diverse possibilities. Depth support (sustained development of a specific concept) remains a design gap for future systems.

05

Validate Resistance as Productive Engagement

Design rejection as a first-class interaction. Make it as easy to dismiss a suggestion as to adopt one. Build in space for users to articulate why something doesn't work. The goal isn't maximizing AI adoption, it's maximizing useful exploration, whether that leads to incorporation or informed rejection. The right metric is whether the interaction expanded what the user ultimately created or decided, not how often they followed what AI suggested.

Case Studies

Case Study 1 — Material Expansion

This participant used the Collage Collaborator to expand their material palette and lean into a "fun, whimsical vibe." After exploring the generated variations, they selectively incorporated the iridescent sheet and feather from the New Materials prompt — alongside their own additions of a googly eye, buttons, ribbon, and gems. The AI suggestion acted as a springboard: it surfaced materials they hadn't considered, but the participant decided what to incorporate, where, and why.

This is adaptation: the participant credited the AI directly and implemented specific suggestions. But they also exercised clear creative judgment: they chose not to use the custom-generated collage as a blueprint, only as inspiration for individual elements.

"I used one of the given, automated collages made by the collage co-creator. It inspired me to add items onto my collage that I hadn't thought of earlier."

Case Study 2 — Reinterpretation as Authorship

This participant used the system not for material ideas but as a compositional preview mechanism, a way to see what the empty green margins of their collage could look like before committing. The AI suggested bright, sparkly botanical imagery. The participant used that as a negative reference: seeing the space filled confirmed it could carry visual weight, but they chose a darker, more personal direction: trees, birds, organic magazine cutouts.

This is reinterpretation. The AI suggestion didn't provide the answer; it helped the participant clarify what they actually wanted. The system functioned as a thinking tool, not a design tool.

"I wanted to put stuff in the margins but I wasn't sure how it'd look, it was helpful to see how it would look with the AI and realize I want something similar but a bit different than what it generated."

Conclusions & Broader Implications

This study demonstrates that generative AI can scaffold divergent thinking without causing design fixation: a result that directly contradicts prior work. The key mechanism isn't the AI model itself; it's the design decision to maintain clear separation between what AI suggests and what the user implements.

The broader implication for any product team building AI-powered features: AI works best as a thinking partner that expands the space of what users consider, not a system that narrows it toward a single recommended output. This applies well beyond collage making; to personalization systems, recommendation engines, AI writing tools, and any experience where the risk is that AI suggestion displaces rather than enriches human decision-making.

The success metric shouldn't be how often users follow AI suggestions. It should be whether the interaction expanded what they ultimately created or decided, and whether they felt the outcome was still genuinely theirs.
