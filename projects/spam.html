Work Research About

SPAM!

Streamlining campus communication for event discovery and civic engagement.

Role & Team

UX Designer and UX Researcher in a team of 4

Timeline

7 weeks

Tools

Figma
Balsamiq
Google Workspace

Methods

Contextual interviews, personas, task analysis, wireframing, prototyping, usability testing

Challenge

Campus event and election information was scattered across posters, word of mouth, social media, and separate tools, making opportunities easy to miss and difficult to act on.

Outcome

Designed a mobile prototype that turns passive discovery into a more persistent, personalized, and actionable experience for finding events, voting, and engaging in campus life.

Challenge

Campus event information is fragmented across posters, word of mouth, and social media, leading to missed events and low follow-through.

How might we increase civic participation and transparency in student life by transforming campus “spam culture” into a digital, interactive system?

Existing systems like WeEngage, 25Live, and Google Calendar were siloed. Spam! aimed to bring these disconnected moments of discovery, action, and accountability into one cohesive experience.

Approach

Research Goals

  • Understand how students currently discover and remember campus events
  • Identify barriers to participation and organizational transparency
  • Determine effective motivational and filtering features for engagement apps

Needfinding & Interviews

To understand how students currently discover and act on campus opportunities, we conducted contextual interviews with students, faculty, and alumni. We focused on how people find events, what causes them to miss opportunities, and what kinds of features would make participation feel more manageable and worthwhile.

Key Insights

  • Discovery was fragmented: students relied on posters, word of mouth, email, and social media, with no single reliable source of truth.
  • Intent did not always become action: many students noticed events but forgot details later or lacked a clear next step.
  • Relevance mattered: students wanted filtering by timing, event type, and incentives like free food or leadership opportunities.
“I see a poster, think ‘I want to go,’ but then I forget the date completely.”
“It’d be great to have one place for everything — not 10 different lists.”
“If I could just filter by what’s happening soon or what I actually care about, I’d go to more events.”

Personas & Opportunity Areas

From research, we identified three recurring user needs: faster event discovery, better follow-up with organizations, and greater transparency in leadership and voting flows.

Wendy — Event Discovery

Social connection Timeboxed Filter-first

Snapshot. Friday was blocked for a hangout, but plans fell through. Wendy still wants a social, low-effort plan that fits her saved time slot.

Goal. Find a fun, non-academic event, ideally with free food, happening Friday evening.

Opportunity. Her flow highlighted the need for strong filtering, timely recommendations, and quick RSVP actions.

Emily — Org Follow-Up

First-year Exploration Asks & Contact

Snapshot. After sampling many events, Emily is deciding which orgs to join. She recently tried BOW Climbing and wants more details.

Goal. Get questions answered and contact someone she met at the event.

Opportunity. Her journey showed that joining an organization is not a single event interaction. She needed ways to revisit information, ask questions, and contact people after attending.

Sofia — Leadership & Voting

Two-year member Leadership Accountability

Snapshot. Long-time member of Counterpoint magazine who wants to step into leadership next year.

Goal. Discover open roles, submit an SOI, and vote with confidence.

Opportunity. Her flow emphasized the need for transparent election information, visible timelines, and clear confirmation states when submitting materials or voting.

Design Goals

OBJECTIVES

  1. Increase visibility of campus events, elections, and civic opportunities
  2. Reduce friction in key actions like browsing, RSVPing, and voting
  3. Support trust through clear confirmations, timelines, and outcomes
  4. Encourage ongoing participation through lightweight motivation and personalization

DESIGN GUIDELINES

  1. Accessible: use high contrast, legible layouts, and low cognitive load
  2. Energetic: retain the playfulness of campus spam culture without visual clutter
  3. Motivating: encourage participation through lightweight rewards and feedback
  4. Transparent: make ownership, timelines, and outcomes visible across flows

Information Architecture & Core Flows

Task & Domain Analysis

  • Find and RSVP to civic events filtered by interest or timing
  • Contact orgs for leadership or follow-up questions
  • Vote in campus or org elections with transparent confirmation

These tasks shaped the core structure of the product around four primary tabs: Events, Voting, Resources, and Profile.

Early navigation and interaction sketches, later recreated in Balsamiq, helped us validate the tab model, test filtering affordances, and refine copy before moving into visual styling.

Usability Evaluation

We developed a clickable prototype in Balsamiq and a high-fidelity Figma mockup for final presentation. The layout used modular blocks and generous white space for readability while maintaining the vibrancy of campus life.

Testing

Usability testing included in-class pilots and remote moderated sessions. Participants completed three core tasks:

  • RSVP to an event
  • Cast a vote in a student election
  • Upload a Statement of Intent for leadership

Findings

  • Navigation and filtering were rated intuitive and engaging.
  • Users praised visual clarity and consistent color coding.
  • Some onboarding steps required clearer vote confirmation cues.

Iterations Based on Testing

  • Simplified landing page with onboarding text
  • Added clearer confirmation for votes and RSVPs
  • Introduced badges for engagement and event attendance
  • Improved hierarchy for mobile readability
  • Added a “My Results” transparency page
  • Clarified the SSO authentication pathway
  • Added notification preferences and privacy disclaimers

Reflection & Next Steps

  • Visibility drives participation: Centralized information increased event engagement.
  • Transparency creates trust: Visible confirmation and results improved voting confidence.
  • Gamification works when balanced: Badges motivated but did not distract.
  • Accessibility through simplicity: A limited palette and hierarchy improved cognitive flow.

Future Improvements

  • Integrate authentication and secure data infrastructure
  • Allow custom notifications for events and elections
  • Run longitudinal usability studies to track engagement

Spam! demonstrates how HCI principles — needfinding, prototyping, and iterative testing — can transform everyday campus systems into accessible civic tools that inspire active participation and accountability.
